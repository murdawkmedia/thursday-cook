Thursday is a set of ideas around using lessons from aviation to make life and technology better for people.
why should a 21-year-old college student, a 30-year-old office worker, and a 55-year-old healthcare worker
have to use technology the same way?

Technology should learn the way we work and make small changes to make _that_ easier, instead of forcing us to learn it.
We should strive to build systems that work well when the humans in it are having a bad day.

thursday asks: what tiny tweak can we make to gather some info about how the user works?

can we do some quick checks to narrow down the possibilities?

- Are they rested?
- Are they nourished?
- what is their goal right now? What's the tiniest thing we can do to make a change in the situation towards the goal?
- Do you need to keep anyone informed about the situation/goal?

We're trying to find the tiniest thing to reduce the pressure to act a bit, keep the plane flying, and plan for what's happening next.

it may not need to be tech at first, you can start with tiny lifestyle tweaks. meet people where they're at. implementing a 30-minute period of focused work.

The aviation lessons:

Duty limitations - Pilots have limitations on the number of hours they can fly in a month and minimum rest periods before flights. Operator fatigue can very quickly throw the balance

Data-driven design - Test early. Test often. Make the minimum reversible change you can to gather some data, then act based on it.

Sterile zones - Pilots have designated periods in the cockpit during critical phases of flight like takeoff and landing, where they reduce nonessential work and chatter to a minimum to focus on the task at hand.

Flying is the world's safest mode of transportation. Every year, airlines move billions of people across the planet with almost no minor incident. One of the key reasons is this data-driven, measured approach - instead of blaming, ask, which factors contributed to this failure? What can we do to minimize the risk next time?

Thursday is like flight school for your life.

Why did early ai coding agents feel so addictive? i think it was because they were still too limited to
work autonomously. you had to still maintain quite a good awareness and understanding of the codebase
and work through the code edit by edit, but the mechanical parts were automated away. it was easy to
get the agent to sneeze out a script to mechanize something. so it felt like thinking. As coding agents got
better and better, they moved the user further and further from the fun problem solving, the prototyping
and design. but we were too entrenched in slop to go back to the fun way. most of the time AI will give you
a design that's good enough early on, and suddenly you've built a huge fix backlog. So, prototype early,
prototype fast. test frequently, test extensively. benchmark and gather data! you're not a solo dev anymore.
you've got an army of robots working for you.
